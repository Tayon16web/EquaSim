Derivations

Where the equations come from, worked from first principles rather than just stated.

From the definition of acceleration

Start from the definition of (constant) acceleration as the rate of change of velocity, and integrate up.

$$a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \int_{v_0}^{v} dv' = \int_0^t a\,dt' \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; v = v_0 + at$$

Integrate velocity once more to get position, since v = dx/dt:

$$x = x_0 + \int_0^t v(t')\,dt' = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$$
Eliminating time — solve the velocity equation for t = (v-v₀)/a and substitute into the position equation; after simplifying, the time-independent relation falls out:
$$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x-x_0)$$

This last form is useful whenever a problem never asks "how long," only "how far" or "how fast."

Work–energy theorem

Work done by the net force along a path is defined as the line integral of force over displacement. Substitute Newton's second law and use the chain rule to trade dt for dv:

$$W = \int F\,dx = \int m\dfrac{dv}{dt}\,dx = \int m\,\dfrac{dx}{dt}\,dv = \int_{v_0}^{v} mv'\,dv' = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2$$

So net work equals the change in kinetic energy — this is exact for any force, constant or not, as long as it's the net force.

Gravitational PE from work against gravity

Near Earth's surface, the work needed to lift a mass a height h against the (locally constant) gravitational force is stored as potential energy:

$$U = -\int_0^h (-mg)\,dy = mgh$$
This is a local approximation. The exact gravitational potential energy, valid at any distance, is U = -GMm/r; expanding it for small height changes near a planet's surface recovers mgh up to an additive constant.

Spring potential energy

For a Hookean spring, F = -kx. Work done against the spring while stretching it from 0 to x:

$$U = \int_0^x kx'\,dx' = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$$

Impulse–momentum theorem

Starting again from Newton's second law in its more fundamental momentum form and integrating over time directly (no chain-rule trick needed this time, since force is already a function of t):

$$F = \dfrac{dp}{dt} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \int_{t_1}^{t_2} F\,dt = \int_{p_1}^{p_2} dp \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; J = \Delta p$$

This is why impulse — the time-integral of force — is the natural quantity for collisions: forces during impact are large but brief and often poorly known, while the net momentum change is measurable.

Conservation of momentum

For an isolated system of two bodies, Newton's third law gives F₁₂ = -F₂₁ at every instant. Integrating both sides over the same time interval:

$$\Delta p_1 = -\Delta p_2 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \Delta(p_1+p_2)=0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p_{1,i}+p_{2,i} = p_{1,f}+p_{2,f}$$

Total momentum is conserved precisely because internal forces cancel in pairs — this generalizes to any number of bodies with only internal forces acting.

The linear → rotational dictionary

Every rotational equation below is the linear equation with x → θ, v → ω, a → α, m → I, F → τ, p → L. This works because each rotational quantity is defined by the same calculus operation as its linear counterpart, just performed on angle instead of position.

Why mass becomes moment of inertia: for a rigid body, kinetic energy is the sum over all mass elements of their individual linear KE, but each element's speed is v = ωr:
$$KE_{rot} = \sum_i \tfrac{1}{2}m_i v_i^2 = \sum_i \tfrac{1}{2}m_i (\omega r_i)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\omega^2 \sum_i m_i r_i^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$$

The factor Σmᵢrᵢ² is defined as the moment of inertia I — it plays exactly the role mass plays in linear KE, which is why the whole linear toolkit carries over term-for-term.

Simple harmonic motion from Hooke's law

Newton's second law for a mass on an ideal spring, with no other forces:

$$m\ddot{x} = -kx \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \ddot{x} + \dfrac{k}{m}x = 0$$

This is the classic linear, constant-coefficient second-order ODE. Guessing a solution of the form x = A cos(ωt + φ) and substituting back:

$$-A\omega^2\cos(\omega t+\varphi) + \dfrac{k}{m}A\cos(\omega t+\varphi) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \omega^2 = \dfrac{k}{m} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \omega = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}$$

The amplitude A and phase φ are set by initial conditions; the angular frequency is fixed entirely by the physics of the system, which is the defining feature of SHM.

The same equation form — restoring "force" proportional to negative displacement — is why the small-angle pendulum (θ̈ + (g/L)θ = 0) and LC circuits obey the same mathematics with different symbols.
